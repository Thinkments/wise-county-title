## The Persistent Problem of Unreleased Historical Mortgages

One of the most frustrating title clouds encountered on Schedule C of a Texas Title Commitment is an **unreleased Deed of Trust** from 20, 30, or 40 years ago.

Often, the original homeowner paid off their 15-year or 30-year mortgage in full, but the lender failed to record a formal **Release of Lien** in the county records. Decades later, when the property is sold or inherited, the original lending bank may have gone bankrupt, merged multiple times, or dissolved completely (e.g., historical savings & loans or defunct mortgage brokers).

---

## The Statutory Solution: Texas CPRC §16.035

Rather than requiring property owners to spend thousands of dollars on lengthy Quiet Title lawsuits in District Court, Texas statute provides a clear, self-executing mechanism to extinguish stale liens.

### Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code §16.035(a) & (d):
> *"A person must bring suit for the recovery of real property under a real property lien or the foreclosure of a real property lien not later than **four years** after the day the cause of action accrues."*
>
> *"On the expiration of the four-year period, the real property lien and a power of sale to enforce the real property lien **become void**."*

## When Does the 4-Year Clock Start?

1. **If Maturity Date is Stated in the Deed of Trust:** The 4-year statute of limitations begins running on the exact maturity date stated on the face of the recorded deed of trust (or the maturity date of the most recently recorded extension agreement).
2. **If NO Maturity Date is Stated:** Under Texas CPRC §16.035(d), if the deed of trust does not state a maturity date, the limitation period is **40 years** from the date the deed of trust was recorded.
